# Mechanics by layer

One act per element. Each act introduces exactly one new verb and reuses every
verb before it, so the final composition uses all six at once.

Notation below: **Hear / Find / Change / Make** are the four beats of the core
loop described in `game-design.md`.

---

## Act 1 — Rhythm (The Escapement)

Teacher: **Toma**. Verb: **Pulse**.

The chamber is the Orchestrion's clockwork: an escapement, a pendulum, gear
trains. Rhythm is the only element that is visibly mechanical, which is why it
comes first — the player can see what they are hearing.

### Mechanics

**1.1 Finding the pulse.** A piece plays. The player taps along. The gear
begins to turn only when taps agree with the beat. Crucially the player must
also find the **downbeat**, not just the tempo — the gear turns but the chamber
only lights when tap 1 lands on beat 1.

**1.2 Meter.** Same passage, three gear sizes: 2, 3, 4. The player picks the
gear that fits. Waltz vs. march vs. compound time, felt rather than counted.

**1.3 Subdivision.** A beat is a stone on the path. The player splits stones
into halves, thirds, quarters. Walking across plays the rhythm. Uneven splits
produce the swing/triplet feel and the player hears the difference immediately.

**1.4 Syncopation.** The mechanic that teaches the most. A row of accent
weights sits on the beats. The player drags a weight **off** the beat. The gear
lurches, then finds a new groove. Copland's point — that syncopation is
defined by the beat it displaces — is unarguable once you've dragged the weight
and heard the pull.

**1.5 Polyrhythm.** Two gears, 3 teeth and 2 teeth, on one axle. Player sets
both spinning and finds where they align. This is the act's "harder ear."

### Commission

_"Eight bars for two drums. Give me a groove I can walk to, and put one
surprise in it."_ Constraints checked: a consistent meter, at least one
syncopated accent, and a return to the downbeat at the end.

### Props needed

Pendulum, escapement gears (2/3/4 tooth), beat stones, accent weights,
rest tokens, time-signature dials, Toma's frame drum, walking-path tiles.

## Act 2 — Melody (The Long Stair)

Teacher: **Sable**. Verb: **Trace**.

A staircase spiralling up the chamber. Height is pitch. The whole act is
literally about going somewhere and coming back.

### Mechanics

**2.1 Contour.** The player draws a freehand line with the cursor; it becomes a
melody. No notation, no note names. This is the first thing they do in the act,
before any theory, so melody is understood as _shape_ first.

**2.2 Steps and leaps.** The stair has close treads and wide gaps. Walking a
step is a second; jumping a gap is a leap. The player reconstructs a heard
melody by choosing steps or leaps — training interval recognition without ever
using the word "minor sixth."

**2.3 Question and answer.** Sable sings a four-bar antecedent. The player must
build a consequent that _answers_ it: same rhythm, different ending, resolving
downward. Wrong answers are not marked wrong; Sable simply repeats her phrase,
implying the player left her hanging.

**2.4 The climax.** The act's key idea and its namesake. A melody is displayed
as a full arc. The player places its high point. Move it to the middle: dull.
Move it too early: the phrase sags. Place it around two thirds through and the
line suddenly has shape. Copland's "long line" demonstrated in one drag.

**2.5 Inversion and sequence.** Take a motif; flip it, shift it up a step,
repeat it. The player discovers how far a tiny idea travels.

### Commission

_"A melody of sixteen bars. One idea. I want to know where its peak is without
you telling me."_ Constraints: a single recurring motif, one clear high point
outside the middle four bars, and a final phrase that comes to rest.

### Props needed

Spiral stair treads, pitch lanterns, contour ribbon, interval gap-planks,
call/answer bridge, climax marker (a flag or bell), motif seed, Sable's lantern.

## Act 3 — Harmony (The Vault)

Teacher: **Quint**. Verb: **Stack**.

An underground vault with a beacon at the center: the tonic. Harmony is taught
as _place_ — where you are, how far from home, and how you get back.

### Mechanics

**3.1 Stacking.** Drop a root, then stack thirds. Two notes: bare. Three: a
chord. The player hears major and minor as physically different stack heights.

**3.2 Home and away.** The tonic beacon glows. The player walks the vault; as
they move to other chords the light dims and the harmony grows restless.
Distance from home is audible and visible simultaneously.

**3.3 Cadences as doors.** Locked doors open only on a resolution. A perfect
cadence throws the bolt cleanly; a deceptive cadence rattles it and the door
stays shut — the sound of the surprise _is_ the feedback. Plagal and half
cadences open different doors, so the player learns cadence types by which
route they take.

**3.4 Tension.** A dissonance meter. The player deliberately builds tension
with suspensions and sevenths and then releases it. Quint's lesson: dissonance
is not error, it is the reason resolution feels like anything.

**3.5 Modulation.** A key-ring mechanic on a circle-of-fifths dial. The player
pivots to a neighbouring key and the whole chamber re-tints. Returning home
after modulating is the act's most satisfying single moment.

### Commission

_"A progression that leaves home and comes back. I want to feel the leaving."_
Constraints: establish a tonic, reach at least one non-adjacent key area, and
resolve with a cadence of the player's choosing.

### Props needed

Chord crystals (major/minor/diminished/seventh), tonic beacon, cadence doors,
circle-of-fifths dial, key rings, tension meter, suspension weights, Quint's
portative organ.

## Act 4 — Tone color (The Cabinet)

Teacher: **Bell**. Verb: **Voice**.

A workshop of hanging instruments. The same melody, over and over, in different
voices — because that is the only honest way to teach timbre.

### Mechanics

**4.1 Blind identification.** An instrument plays behind a screen. The player
picks it from the rack. Families first (strings, winds, brass, percussion),
then instruments, then registers.

**4.2 Register.** One instrument, three registers. A clarinet's low chalumeau
and its high register sound like different creatures, and the player is asked
to notice that before being told why.

**4.3 Orchestration.** The player's own act 2 melody returns, and they assign
it to an instrument. Then to a different one. Then to two at once. The act's
core insight: the notes did not change and the meaning did.

**4.4 Blend and contrast.** Combine two voices. Some fuse into a new color;
some stay stubbornly separate. The player builds a small palette and Bell
comments on each pairing like a matchmaker.

**4.5 Playing technique.** Mutes, pizzicato, tremolo, stopped horn. Modifier
tokens the player clips onto an instrument.

### Commission

_"Take a melody you already wrote. Score it for three instruments so I can hear
all three."_ Constraints: three distinct timbres, at least one register change,
and no voice masked by another.

### Props needed

Instrument sprites by family (violin, cello, flute, clarinet, oboe, horn,
trumpet, trombone, timpani, harp, piano), instrument rack, screen, spectral
prism, register ladder, mute/pizzicato/tremolo tokens, Bell's workbench.

## Act 5 — Texture (The Loomworks)

Teacher: **Odile**. Verb: **Weave**.

A loom. Each voice is a thread. This is the most direct metaphor in the game
and it is doing real work: "texture" is already the word musicians use.

### Mechanics

**5.1 One thread.** Monophony. A single line, unaccompanied. The player hears
how exposed it is.

**5.2 Thread and ground.** Homophony. Melody plus accompaniment. The player
sets which thread is foreground; the loom renders one bright, others muted.

**5.3 Independent threads.** Polyphony. Two melodies at once, neither
subordinate. The player must follow one while the other plays — the game asks
them to trace only the second voice, which is genuinely hard and genuinely
teachable.

**5.4 Canon.** Start the same thread later. The player sets the delay and the
interval, and the loom shows the diagonal pattern that results. Round-singing,
made visual.

**5.5 Counterpoint rules as loom constraints.** Threads that collide (parallel
fifths, crossed voices) snag the shuttle. Rather than a theory lecture, the
player feels the friction and adjusts spacing.

### Commission

_"Two voices that need each other. Neither one is the servant."_ Constraints:
both lines independently singable, contrary motion at least twice, no snags.

### Props needed

Loom frame, shuttle, thread spools by voice color, warp/weft grid, canon
delay-pegs, snag markers, finished cloth swatches (mono/homo/polyphonic),
Odile's shears.

## Act 6 — Form (The Nave)

Teacher: **Feld**. Verb: **Build**.

A cathedral under construction. Form is architecture: sections are rooms, and
the listener walks through them in time.

### Mechanics

**6.1 Sections as blocks.** The player's own material becomes labelled blocks.
Arrange A B A. Hear it. Arrange A B A C A — a rondo. Repetition is revealed as
the thing that makes length comprehensible.

**6.2 Variation.** One theme, many transformations: change the rhythm, the
mode, the register, the ornament, the harmony beneath it. The player builds a
set of five variations and Feld points out which ones still recognisably carry
the theme and which have wandered off.

**6.3 Fugue.** A subject enters. The player must place the answer at the right
pitch and the right moment, then the countersubject beneath it. Presented as
stacked arches that only hold weight if the entries are correctly spaced.

**6.4 Sonata form.** The act's capstone, and a walkable map rather than a
diagram. Exposition (two contrasting rooms, first and second subject),
development (a maze where the material is fragmented and re-lit), recapitulation
(the same two rooms, now in the same key), coda (the door out). The player
literally travels the form.

**6.5 Free form.** Feld's final lesson: he dismantles a wall and asks the player
to justify a structure that follows no template. Some shapes are argued, not
inherited.

### Commission

_"Build me something with three rooms. I should know when I've walked into a
new one, and I should know when I'm home."_ Constraints: at least three
sections, one returning section, and an audible transition between each.

### Props needed

Form blocks (A/B/C), blueprint table, arch pieces for fugue entries, scaffold,
sonata-form floor map, keystone, variation stamps (rhythm/mode/ornament),
Feld's plumb line and mallet.

## Act 7 — The Orchestrion

No teacher. The player's accumulated material from all six commissions is laid
out. The final commission is open:

_"Something of your own. Use what you have. It should be worth the walk."_

The only hard constraint is duration (roughly 90 seconds). The Orchestrion
plays it back with full orchestration, and the six teachers listen. Each of
them responds to whichever element the player leaned on hardest — so the ending
is different depending on what kind of composer the player became.

### Scoring, such as it is

The game reports back in prose, not numbers:

> "You leaned on rhythm. Toma noticed. Sable thinks your second phrase asked a
> question you never answered — she is not wrong, but I liked it anyway."
